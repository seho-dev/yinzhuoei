I have a lot on my mind today and want to jot down a few thoughts, mostly about where to go next. I have been working in frontend development for about two and a half years and have learned quite a range of things, but I want a clearer direction. My benchmark is reaching the level of a senior engineer in Singapore or at a leading Chinese tech company in two or three years.

---

Frontend development spans several areas. One is the application implementation layer, which is where most frontend and backend developers in China work. On the frontend, for example, knowing JavaScript, CSS, and a few UI frameworks or view libraries is enough to implement requirements. If you have begun to think in terms of software engineering, you might also design components and use third-party technologies such as Lit or EJS, along with related plugins and tools. Other parts of this layer include mini programs, lightweight apps, and native platforms. The platforms and technologies vary, but the work is still fundamentally about implementing applications. I have always wanted to move beyond that layer. Whenever I saw people on Zhihu or in WeChat groups discussing the difference between junior, mid-level, and senior engineers, I would quietly try to place myself on that scale. Looking back, that was rather naive. A programmer's ability is not just about coding; it also includes engineering, architecture, communication, and even product management and operations.

I am getting sidetracked. Back to the different areas.

I will skip the purely presentational layer—the name speaks for itself: visual effects built with things like CSS and Canvas.

The application architecture layer immediately brings exceptional programmers to mind. Becoming an architect or CTO is every programmer's ultimate dream. Architecture tests both the breadth and depth of a developer's knowledge. Most of the people you see in this area are a bit older, many born in the 1980s or early 1990s. Computer science topics such as compiler theory and algorithms start to matter here. You establish conventions, optimize underlying systems, and design application and framework architectures. You are solving genuinely technical problems. My experience in this area is still very, very limited, but it is a direction I am continuing to pursue.

Then there is the infrastructure layer. Over the past two years, infrastructure has been a major theme in conference talks, particularly those from ByteDance. You are most likely to find engineers specializing in it at large companies. This layer covers automated builds, loading, performance optimization, code quality, and tools or platforms that help other developers work. My exposure here is also quite limited. My work on automated builds, performance, and code quality has mostly been small-scale tinkering, either on my own or with a small team. Still, I am doing it. Building infrastructure is difficult without the right environment and resources.

Outside my main specialty, I am studying Ruby, some Node.js, serverless, and operations-related topics. I have also been putting together an open-source team, starting small and gradually building up. Working on products, operations, frontend, and backend has honestly left me a little torn about my direction, but writing this post has helped clear my head. First, I need to remember why I started: frontend is still my main focus. I want to develop broadly toward architecture, then infrastructure. And I absolutely need to get my English up to scratch…

Good night. Time for bed.
