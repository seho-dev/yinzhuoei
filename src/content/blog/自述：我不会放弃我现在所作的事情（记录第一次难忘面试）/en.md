I recently turned 18, and some friends I had not heard from in a long time sent me birthday wishes. Let me look back briefly on the past few years.

Many of you already know my story: I dropped out in my final year of high school to learn programming, before I had even turned 17. Around the age of 15, I started getting involved in the internet's gray-market businesses. I wrote scripts with a few older guys to earn a little money and feed my teenage vanity.

The turning point came after I got home from school one day. My routine was to close the study door, take my books out of my bag, and turn on the computer, pretending to do homework. In reality, I spent every day following industry news, writing scripts, and updating software. Whenever my mom came in, I would pretend to be studying. It could not go on forever. One evening, I told her the truth: I wanted to study computing properly. That is how I ended up in a vocational college program in Xi'an.

Before that, I thought my technical skills were ahead of those of people my age, and I felt rather pleased with myself. Reality hit hard: I ended up wasting almost a year. Without a learning path, I felt completely lost. In 2018, I shut down all my business activities and kept only the blog. In June, I started learning frontend development and Java. I took my laptop everywhere, went to the library to look things up, watched videos, and read official documentation entirely in English. That was when I improved the most. In just a few months, I made many good friends, some still learning and others already working as programmers. Whatever they were discussing always seemed to be the next thing I needed to learn. My stack at the time was Vue and Java.

Then came another turning point. Our two-year program was nearing graduation, and many classmates started looking for jobs in testing, operations, and similar fields. I wanted to try too. With my limited view of the industry, I decided to test the waters and sent my résumé—18 years old, vocational college—to three small companies in Xi'an. Only one invited me to interview.

I went with a good friend. He was 21, had served in the military, and had formally studied vanilla JavaScript. We arrived at a studio inside a fairly large residential complex, a small team of about seven people. The boss was not there when we walked in. Fine, I could deal with that. A PHP developer spoke with me. She said they had a Vue project and wanted someone experienced to guide them, since they were learning it too. I was confused, and so was my friend. What was this supposed to be? Were they kidding? Then she asked, “Do you know PHP?” I said I had studied it for a couple of days and could get up to speed within a week if needed. “So you only know Java?” she asked. I said yes. Then: “Do you know the Nuxt framework?” The conversation did not sound particularly professional. I said yes; I had just read the documentation the day before. After thinking for a moment, she said, “Two frontend candidates came in this morning and are waiting to hear back. Leave your résumé here, and we will contact you if an internship opens up.” (A 99% chance that was the end of it.)

There were several reasons the interview was awkward: I had prepared in a rush, I was only 17 at the time and could not be hired, my résumé was thrown together, and it did not fully describe my project experience. Still, the interview helped me enormously by giving me a sense of direction. As I walked out that door, my confidence reignited, along with a burning desire to prove myself. Over the next two months, I learned a great deal and made plans for the coming year and the Lunar New Year holiday.

I recently fixed a Java bug that had kept me stuck for two days. Even my teacher had no solution, and I was on the verge of a breakdown. I am writing this as a reminder to myself: do not give up on what you are doing. Technical skill can overcome doubts about your age. But if you stay immature in your thinking, that really is the end of the road.

It has been a long time since my last update. I have written lots of notes recently; readers can download Evernote so we can share them and study together.
