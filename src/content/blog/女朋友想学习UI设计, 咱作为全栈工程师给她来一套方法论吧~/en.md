As programmers, we have a relatively easy time learning new things, especially in the tech industry we already know. We often encounter information that takes our peers in other roles much longer to discover, thanks to that interesting thing called an “information gap.” So when my girlfriend wanted to learn UI design from scratch, I felt right in my element. As a longtime frontend developer, I spend a great deal of my working day talking—or battling—with designers. In this article, I will organize my thoughts on learning UI design, explain the concepts, look at what UI designers actually need to know in tech companies, and discuss the salaries everyone is curious about. Designers reading this: your feedback is very welcome!

## Understanding the Role

Many people are unfamiliar with terms such as UI and UX, which leads to misunderstandings. Some imagine UI/UX designers are like interior designers who visit construction sites and produce engineering drawings. It is a bit like the stereotypes that all programmers are “hackers,” “account thieves,” or people in plaid shirts. Whether you are a beginner trying to understand UI/UX or explaining the job to an outsider, you need to understand what the role actually involves. I think this is crucial—more important, at least, than learning the software.

### UI

UI stands for “user interface.” As a programmer, the word “interface” immediately gets my attention. Think of it as the means of communication between a user—a person—and an interface—a product. Related ideas, UI in a broader sense, existed long before electronic devices and screens. They fall under “ergonomics,” a discipline centered on how people interact and communicate with machines and their environment. Look closely, and the connection is clear. During World War II, fighter cockpits often put every control directly in front of the pilot. Operating errors sometimes caused crashes, and people gradually realized that communication between users and machines needed to be smoother and easier. This helped give rise to ergonomics as a discipline. Today, we mostly interact with electronic devices. The websites, apps, and system applications we use need that same improvement in communication. That is the UI designer's responsibility.

### UX

UX stands for “user experience.” In Chinese, I think the term emphasizing the lived experience is more fitting than the one suggesting accumulated knowledge. Most job listings discuss UI and UX together. UI & UX design means adjusting both the flow and the appearance to suit a particular audience. From a frontend engineer's perspective, UX can encompass animation, gestures, typography, layout, navigation, and so on. These design decisions should come from studying specific user groups, not simply keeping your head down and producing graphics. At larger companies, you may see dedicated UX positions. These generally require skills such as studying user psychology and analyzing product usability, with less emphasis on visual design, or UI.

### Choosing a Direction

If you would rather design beautiful interfaces than focus on user psychology and product analysis, UI is the direction for you. A good UI designer still needs basic UX skills, though. Conversely, if interface visuals do not interest you as much as user behavior, and you are not worried about finding a suitable job, you can focus on UX.

## The Basic Structure of a Tech Product Team

If you have not entered the tech industry yet, you probably do not know exactly what a designer does on a team. Here is a brief overview. Take an app as an example: there is a side that sets requirements and a side that delivers the product. The stakeholders—usually the boss and other managers—determine the app's overall direction and business scenarios.

For example, Boss Shen says, “Next quarter, we are building an app called [Little Green Book]. Just copy Xiaohongshu—Little Red Book.”

A product manager, or PM, then organizes the boss's requirements and direction into written documents and diagrams that communicate them to designers and developers. This step is critical. The PM's output must closely match what the boss wants and receive the boss's approval. Otherwise, design and development will work from the wrong requirements, potentially bringing the whole project to a halt.

Liu, the PM, says, “Got it, boss. We have confirmed the requirements, so I will go meet with design and development.”

The designer uses the PM's output to design the app's UI and connect its pages—for example, specifying that page A navigates to page B. Designers do not normally jump straight into drawing screens. They research the background, target audience, and competing products, then discuss several UI versions with the PM and boss. Only once a final decision is made does the design reach the developers.

Lei, the designer, says, “Hao, the UI is finalized in **Figma**, a collaborative UI design tool. You can start development now.”

Hao, the frontend developer, takes the design and gets to work. Another key role at this stage is the backend developer. Frontend and backend form the core of development: the frontend turns the design into code and communicates with the backend to make the app usable—for example, enabling login, likes, and comments. The backend developer implements the services behind those actions.

The “full-stack engineer” in this article's title broadly means a developer who knows multiple technologies, not necessarily limited to frontend and backend. Once the developers finish writing the code, QA engineers check its quality. Plenty of bugs turn up during testing, so QA works with frontend and backend developers to improve the product. Finally, once it passes inspection, Little Green Book goes live!

Here is the basic workflow of a tech product team:

Boss / stakeholder -> Product manager -> Designer -> Frontend developer -> Backend developer -> QA engineer

## Which Design Tools Should You Learn?

This section is just my personal opinion. There is no hierarchy of superiority among tools. Consider the hiring demand in your target city and the operating system you use. Here are a few popular design applications:

1. Photoshop, or PS: you know the one. If you use Windows, consider it a required course.
2. Sketch, jokingly pronounced “si-kai-chi” in Chinese: if you use a Mac, this is your required course.
3. Figma, pronounced “fei-ge-ma” in Chinese: a standalone design tool and one of the world's trendiest design platforms, used by major companies in China and abroad. Most importantly, it does not care whether you run Windows or macOS.
4. MasterGo, jokingly pronounced “ma-si-te-er-gou” in Chinese: a Chinese alternative similar to Figma, launched only recently this year. If you like Figma but cannot use it for whatever reason, this is a very good option, especially given the dominance of overseas tools.
5. After Effects, or AE: optional, with a fairly steep learning curve.
6. Cinema 4D, or C4D: optional, also fairly difficult.

My recommendation: just choose Figma. It is similar enough to Sketch that learning one gives you a head start with the other. You can treat Photoshop as an elective.

## What Computer Should You Use?

On Zhihu and other forums, programmers constantly debate whether to use macOS or Windows. I have not really seen the same debate among designers.
Just choose macOS, preferably an M1 MacBook Pro. Do not bother choosing a Mac laptop for its large screen: your company will likely provide an external monitor, or an iMac if it has money to spare. Those have large screens.
If you are learning Figma, there is no need to buy a Mac during your studies. For your career later on, though, your main design machine should be a Mac. Let the company pay for it—why spend your own money?

And for anyone new to this: I mean an Apple Mac computer, not MAC the lipstick brand!

## How to Teach Yourself

If you were thinking about signing up for a training course before reading this, I hope you will reconsider. A course costing RMB 20,000–30,000 could buy you three Mac laptops. Uninstall the games and Douyin from your phone, and spend six months to a year seriously studying on your own. This section explains how. I am a programmer, but the principles are the same. I do not expect you to get up at six and study all day like I do. Two or three hours a day is plenty.

### Habits

You need to develop a few good habits. When you first start learning design, regularly browse design news and articles. There are plenty of sources. Beyond familiar Chinese platforms such as Zhihu, Zcool, UISDC, and Bilibili—no, I am not recommending Huaban or Qiantuwang; spend less time on those—you should follow a few more platforms at home and abroad. Some load slowly; if you have the means, access overseas sites. They matter because they give you firsthand information:

1. https://dribbble.com/ A serious source of inspiration.
2. https://www.pinterest.com/ Fantastic imagery.
3. https://www.behance.net/ A portfolio site full of beautifully designed visual collections.
4. https://www.feeeedin.com A Chinese design-news aggregator for browsing everything in one place.
5. http://www.webdesignfile.com/ Excellent website designs.
6. https://muuuuu.org/ More of the same.
7. https://siiimple.com/ More of the same.
8. https://ui8.net/ More of the same.
9. https://miro.com/signup/ More of the same.

Designers work with frontend developers, so it also helps to know the component libraries developers use: the design systems from major companies such as Alibaba and Tencent, primarily aimed at programmers. As a UI designer, you only need to become familiar with them:

1. https://ant.design/components/button-cn/ Alibaba's Ant Design component library.
2. https://youzan.github.io/vant/#/zh-CN/button Youzan's Vant component library, excellent for mobile.
3. https://semi.design/zh-CN Douyin's Semi component library, fresh and cool.

As a frontend developer, I have selected these three component libraries for you to know. You should also become familiar with icon resources and guidelines. There are many, so here is just one of the best in China:

1. https://www.iconfont.cn/

The same goes for typography. Design work often calls for distinctive fonts, which you can download here. This is also one of the most useful font platforms in China:

1. https://www.hellofont.cn/

The point of all these links is to show you how to find resources. With access to overseas sites, you can explore even more impressive platforms, follow the latest trends, get information firsthand, and turn that information advantage into a professional advantage. Spend half an hour each day browsing these platforms and reading articles. If you have energy left, apply what you discover to your work, summarize what you have learned, and publish it in the community. This builds your community presence, just as it does for programmers.

### Video Resources

Now, this deserves a little thought. Much of learning UI design involves learning how to operate the software. That is also what training courses tend to teach. They generally do not develop your aesthetic judgment; most simply teach you which buttons to press. By now, I hope you have decided which application to learn. Using Figma as an example, here are a couple of introductory tutorial recommendations:

1. https://space.bilibili.com/15741969/channel/collectiondetail?sid=255058 Search Bilibili for “Xiangsu Fan Figma.”
2. https://space.bilibili.com/108104104 A popular Bilibili creator with excellent Figma content.

Beyond software tutorials, I hope you will watch talks and open lectures by well-known designers. You can find them on Geek Time, Juejin, Zhihu, and UISDC. UISDC, in particular, has designers from major companies sharing their working habits. I do not think all of it is immediately useful to beginners, but you will still pick things up.

### Recommended Books

Like programmers, UI designers should read some substantial books. They will not transform your design skills overnight. Instead, they can accompany you throughout your career and help you progress to the next level.

1. The Golden Rules of UI Design.
2. Talking About Designers.
3. The Tencent Story.

### Studying Efficiently on Your Own

This section is not specifically about UI design. These are tools and habits I have found useful while learning programming:

1. Notion: a beautiful, free note-taking tool. Make it your first choice if you can reliably access overseas services.
2. Evernote, wolai, Yuque, or Tencent Docs: wolai is a Chinese alternative to Notion, worth considering if overseas services are inaccessible.
3. Look for video resources on YouTube first; the quality is often higher.
4. Avoid Baidu and use Google. When searching, try to avoid Chinese keywords. You want internationally current design ideas, not secondhand information filtered through local sources.
5. Set a strict schedule: certain hours each day are reserved for study. Nobody should interrupt you, and you should not distract yourself by browsing Douyin or Xiaohongshu.
6. Go to bed early and get up early where possible. Your mind can be very clear at five or six in the morning. If you sleep early enough, you can move those unproductive evening hours to the morning.
7. Organize your browser bookmarks by category, saving the interesting things you discover each day under the appropriate topics.
8. Get into the habit of producing things. Whether videos or articles, and regardless of how good they are, publish them on your blog, social feed, or design platforms. This makes it easier to catch mistakes, gives you a sense of achievement, and lets others point out gaps.
9. Beginner designers do not necessarily need to complete project after project the way programmers do. What matters is exposure to design guidelines across different fields. Competitions and freelance work can improve your skills quickly; you do not always need a complete project.
10. Try joining a few communities: developer circles, designer circles, and assorted chat groups. When questions arise, you will have people to ask. You can also post your questions in public communities.

### A Suggested Schedule

1. 19:00 - 22:00 Learn Figma and take notes.
2. 6:00 - 7:00 Read books.
3. 12:00 - 13:00 Browse the latest industry and technology news.
4. 15:00 - 18:00 Combine last night's notes with your reading and build a few demos around themes you set yourself. Competition briefs work too; UISDC has plenty of competitions, so pick one.

If you have spare energy, read industry news on the subway, on the bus, or during idle moments. UISDC is convenient for this, and its articles really are quite good.

### On Being Called a Tryhard

Do not listen to people who see you studying and immediately accuse you of feeding the “rat race.” I really dislike that label. Keep learning for your own future. Why do people not say the same about those studying for postgraduate entrance exams or civil-service exams? It is a damn double standard; ignore it. You are learning UI design from scratch, so you need a calm, comfortable environment for self-study. Do not let the people around you derail that. If you truly cannot keep going alone, then consider a UI design course. Many of my friends came through programming bootcamps, so I have heard a thing or two about them. Some have very poor learning environments. Instead of helping you build momentum, they may make it harder to focus. Would you believe some people bring their babies along to study UI design? Salespeople are retraining too, so there is plenty of competition.

## Employment Prospects

Most roles in the tech industry are already approaching saturation, and this year's outlook is not encouraging. Here I will briefly describe the job market in Xi'an. For a rough idea of salaries in first-tier cities, add RMB 5,000–8,000 to these figures. Unlike programming jobs, design roles tend to place less emphasis on academic credentials and university prestige, and more on years of experience and practical skills. That is a real advantage for people without particularly strong academic backgrounds. Major companies do have strict educational requirements for UI designers, such as a full-time bachelor's or master's degree, but most ordinary companies do not. Personally, I do not think employees of major companies are inherently better than those elsewhere. Some departments at big companies even do very poor work. If you are just starting out, do not aim unrealistically high or become fixated on a big-name employer. Building your actual skills matters most.

### Salaries

Using Xi'an as an example, and drawing on advice from designer friends, here are some reference ranges. Assuming an educational background somewhere between an associate degree and a master's degree, the monthly figures in RMB look like this:

1. Student intern: 2,000–3,000.
2. One year of experience: 4,000–6,000.
3. Two years of experience: 7,000–8,000.
4. Three years of experience: 10,000–15,000.

In Xi'an's design industry, salaries above RMB 15,000 are hard to reach unless you join a major company or become a design director.
Of course, exceptional ability can take you beyond these ranges. Keep learning every day while working. A single skill is not enough: develop your aesthetic judgment, learn other software, and strengthen both your hard and soft skills.

### Skills Mentioned in Job Descriptions

1. Figma, Illustrator, and Photoshop.
2. Some companies want experience designing large-screen data dashboards. These are very popular in Xi'an.
3. Clear design thinking and solid fundamentals.
4. Some lousy companies ask for HTML, CSS, and JavaScript—frontend skills. Be careful: they may expect you to write code.
5. 3ds Max is a plus.
6. Cinema 4D is a plus.
7. After Effects is a plus.

### Choosing a Good Internship

If you attend an ordinary first- or second-tier university in Xi'an, do not believe promises of “guaranteed employment.” It is 2022; do not fall for that. Learn more on your own and start looking early. Do not wait until graduation is around the corner to begin learning and job hunting—that is asking for trouble. I do recommend campus recruitment, but I usually suggest interviewing with companies independently beforehand for three reasons:

1. More interview practice.
2. Access to better job opportunities.
3. An early sense of your market value.

How should you choose a company? Remember this order:

Major tech company > Company building its own product > Large outsourcing company, such as Chinasoft in Xi'an -> Small outsourcing company

1. Before joining a major company, ask which product and department you will work on. A big-name employer on your résumé can help your future career.
2. A company building its own product means exactly that: regardless of its size, if you think the product is good, it is worth considering.
3. Large outsourcing companies have many projects and usually lots of overtime. One project ends, another begins—it can feel like an electronics factory. Some do not offer good benefits either.
4. Small outsourcing companies often have all the problems of large ones, with even worse benefits. Why bother?

As a newcomer, provided you are not paying to work there, I suggest not getting too hung up on salary unless it is truly unreasonable. My first job after graduation—not an internship—paid RMB 1,200 a month. You can always gain a few months of experience and move on.

### Interview Advice

Prepare how you will present yourself and your résumé, but also put together a polished portfolio to demonstrate your ability. Select from your demos, projects, and competition entries. I will not give specific portfolio recommendations because I have not made one myself, but there are plenty of examples online, particularly on UISDC and Zcool, that explain how to design one.

1. Do not fabricate your résumé. It is dishonest, especially when it comes to age or work experience. Otherwise, you will spend your entire three-month probation worrying about being found out.
2. Be confident. You and the interviewer are equals; do not plead or act submissively.
3. Ask sincerely for interview feedback. A good interviewer may offer useful direction for your next steps.
4. Do not reveal your ideal salary to the interviewer or HR. You have bargained before, right? That is how HR negotiates too. Avoid naming your salary requirements; write “negotiable” on your résumé.
5. Designers generally design their own résumés. If you do not want to, use WonderCV. Please stop making them in Word; those résumés go straight in the bin.
6. Ask about the five social insurance schemes and housing fund, alternating five- and six-day workweeks, overtime pay, and holiday benefits. These are valuable conditions beyond the headline salary.
